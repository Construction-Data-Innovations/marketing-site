The most disciplined, successful GCs I can think of perform their own in-house takeoff and budgets before they've gone out to the market to get prices from specialty trades.

Many GCs, though, wait for sub quotes to come in before they know what a job costs. No judgment. It happens. You broadcast the job files out to your subs, and as you're reading through the project docs [while 2 other fires are burning and the deadline is in 36 hours], you're doing the manual process of transferring what's on the plans to some kind of bid breakdown sheet.

And then you rely on the quantities or scope inclusions from the sub a little too heavily and carry forward with a leaky budget.

When you build your own budget first — trade by trade, before a single quote lands — bid leveling stops being a reconciliation exercise and starts being an audit. You already know what the work should cost. Now you're checking whether the quotes agree.

## Why the Order Matters

Sub quotes arrive at the last minute — it's going to happen. Hopefully, with all our technological progress estimators can dedicate more time to the professional relationship stuff that builds trust over time, and subs give you early pricing, because you as the GC were organized and prepared.

Nonetheless, adrenaline starts to rush, especially when you're still waiting on multiple subs who promised to have their best-and-final prices in 3 hours ago, and the deadline's in 20 minutes.

The process of assembling your final price in the estimating room doesn't have to be a fire drill, though. The GCs who develop their own budget numbers, take-off their own estimated quantities, and engage with subs about the nitty-gritty of their quote long before the bid due date are asking themselves these questions under calm conditions:

1. **Is this quote in scope?** A number that's 15% below your budget might be a better price — or a scope gap that becomes a change order after award.
2. **Is this quote competitive?** Without your own benchmark, you are dependent on the first early budgets you received to set the market. Create your own independent range for each major item of work before comparing the quotes to avoid confusion in the leveling process.

## Building the Budget Trade by Trade

Start with your takeoff. For each trade, derive a rough order-of-magnitude number from the drawings before you send ITBs.

**Concrete:** Square footage of slabs, linear feet of walls, volume of footings. Apply your historical unit cost per SF or CY from comparable jobs.

**Structural steel:** Tons from the structural drawings, multiplied by your installed cost per ton for this building type and location.

**MEP:** These are harder to quantify from early drawings, but you can still budget from system type, building SF, and occupancy. A 40,000 SF office TI has a different MEP profile than a 40,000 SF shell-and-core.

The goal isn't a perfect number. The goal is a defensible number — one you can stand behind when you're comparing it to what comes in.

## What Happens at Bid Leveling

When quotes land and your budget already exists, the conversation changes.

Instead of "which number do we use?" you're asking "why is this number 18% below ours?" Sometimes the answer is a sharper price. More often it's scope missing from their proposal — alternates they excluded, work they assumed someone else would carry, or a material spec they substituted.

Your budget makes those gaps visible.
