<p class="cw-lede">The invitation to bid lands on Monday morning. By Tuesday afternoon someone on your team has opened a new workbook for it, and nobody remembers deciding to.</p>

## Why does every bid get estimated before anyone decides to bid?

Years ago, my team bid a runway for a new airplane hangar as a subcontractor to an ENR 400 general contractor, and while the big logo was attractive, the profit was thin and two red flags showed up early: the GC started micromanaging our means and methods in a trade we know cold, and site access was going to be a challenge with no sign they would help make it any easier. If a GC micromanages you at bid time, they will micromanage you through the whole job, and on a thin margin a hard site with no cooperation leaves nothing for the unexpected, so even with our proposal already in, I pulled it. The sub that took the work got beat up by that GC and turned a minuscule profit, which taught me that walking away is sometimes the best move, because some jobs are not worth the squeeze.

Ideally, you make that call earlier than I did, and the six questions below are a good place to start.

For a VP of Preconstruction, the cost runs past one bid. Every hour spent on an invitation you should have passed on comes out of the hours for one you could have won. The number of bids your group can bid well is capped by the number of estimators who can bid them responsibly, and nobody can clone the best one.

The Associated General Contractors of America and NCCER surveyed 1,830 construction firms in July and August 2026. Among firms with openings for estimating positions, 72 percent reported difficulty filling them.

## What should you ask before spending estimating hours on a bid?

Six questions cover most of what a go/no-go meeting ends up arguing about anyway. I would ask them the day the invitation arrives, and write the answers down.

    <thead>
      <tr><th>Question</th><th>Why it matters</th><th>Hold or pass when</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Do we know this owner or client?</td><td>How an owner or general contractor treats scope, change orders, and payment decides whether a won job keeps its margin.</td><td>You have no history with the owner or client and the contract terms read heavy.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>How far along is the design?</td><td>A thin set forces assumptions, and assumptions nobody wrote down become your cost.</td><td>The set is thin and the owner will not accept written assumptions and exclusions.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Have we built and priced this kind of work before?</td><td>Your own history only helps on work that looks like it.</td><td>The scope sits outside anything you have priced and bought out before.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Who would estimate it, and which bid do they give up?</td><td>Capacity is finite. A yes here is a no somewhere else on the calendar.</td><td>The only Estimator who can carry it is already on a bid you rate higher.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>What did comparable jobs keep?</td><td>Margin lost on similar jobs is the best warning you have for this one.</td><td>You have no record, or the record says the margin faded.</td></tr>
      <tr><td>What would make us walk away?</td><td>A walk-away condition set before the estimate is easier to hold than one set after.</td><td>Nobody in the room can name one, so settle it before anyone opens a workbook.</td></tr>

Most of these answers do not need software. They sit in the heads of two or three people in your office, and the screen's job is to get them onto one page before the estimating hours start.

## Which bids are worth the estimating hours?

Treat two of the questions as knockouts. A heavy-terms owner or client you have no history with is a pass. A bid with no walk-away condition waits until someone writes one down, and passes if no one can.

The other four are a judgment, not a tally. A bid that holds up on most of them goes ahead. A mixed bid goes ahead with conditions written down, such as a cap on estimating hours or a requirement that the next addendum settles a scope question. Put the reason for every pass on the page.

I would not turn this into a weighted score with a threshold. A total looks rigorous and hides the one answer that should have ended the conversation.

<div class="cw-note"><span class="cw-label">Watch out</span><strong>A screen is not a forecast.</strong> It tells you whether a bid is worth building a number for. It does not tell you the number will be right, and a person who knows the owner can still overrule it. Write down the overrule and the reason, so the margin check later can say whether the call held.</div>

## How do you keep the go/no-go consistent across divisions?

If you set the standard for a group, the screen has to read the same in every office. Otherwise one division passes on an owner another division is chasing, and you learn about it from a faded margin instead of a meeting.

Use the same six questions on the same page everywhere. Let each office answer in its own words, but do not let any office skip one.

Decide in advance who can overrule a pass, and put the overrule on the record with the reason. Otherwise a pass can be quietly reversed and nobody learns why.

Then add the question a single-bid screen cannot ask. What are we not bidding this week if we say yes to this one?

Once a quarter, read the pass reasons together. If the same owner or the same thin design stage keeps showing up, that is a risk policy question, and it is yours to answer.

## Did the bids you won keep their margin?

A screen only gets better if you check it against what happened. While a job runs, you can see its projected margin. At closeout, you know the final margin. Set each one beside what the go/no-go record said on the day you decided.

In an ABC member survey conducted October 20 to November 3, 2026, roughly 1 in 4 contractors said they still expect their profit margins to shrink over the next six months. Associated Builders and Contractors published the reading in November 2026.

Start from your own record. In ConstructWise, Bid Performance tracks win rate and margin kept on every job you won, so the look back does not rest on memory. On Podium and up you can also test a unit cost and see the chance it runs over, from your own jobs. That helps when the screen says go with conditions and the condition is a number.

A thin record is still a thin record. If you have tracked only a few jobs, treat the pattern as a question for the next meeting and not as a rule.

## Questions estimators ask

<div class="cw-faq">
  <div class="cw-faq-item"><h3>What is a go/no-go decision in estimating?</h3>It is the decision to bid or pass, made before estimating hours are spent. The useful version is written down with the reason, so you can check it against the job later.</div>
  <div class="cw-faq-item"><h3>Who should be in a go/no-go meeting?</h3>The Preconstruction Leader, the Estimator who would run the bid, the Project Manager or Superintendent from operations who would build it, and whoever owns the owner relationship. The point is to get the people who hold the answers into one room with one page.</div>
  <div class="cw-faq-item"><h3>How long should a go/no-go screen take?</h3>Short enough to finish before anyone opens a workbook. If the screen takes as long as the estimate, nobody will run it.</div>
  <div class="cw-faq-item"><h3>Should we ever bid a job that fails the screen?</h3>Sometimes, for a reason such as a relationship you want to keep. Write the reason down, so the margin check later can tell you whether the reason held.</div>
</div>
